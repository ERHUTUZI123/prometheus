Abstract
Existing models of learning in the brain explain how given circuits learn, but not how biology could assemble those circuits in the first place. To address this gap, we formulate Self-Assembling Learning (SAL)—the study of how learning systems can emerge from lower-level interactions—and introduce one example mechanism, the Self-Assembling Motif (SAM). SAM is self-assembling at two scales: the motif emerges from initially random connectivity under heterosynaptic plasticity rules, and networks of SAMs, composed hierarchically, self-organize into dynamics that provably approximate a generalized form of stochastic gradient descent—matching backpropagation-level performance. This suggests that biological learning need not be prescribed but can emerge from local rules—and to a far greater extent than previously thought.
1 Introduction
Deep neural networks have revolutionized computational neuroscience, where Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are increasingly used to model various aspects of information processing in the brain ([1]; [2]; [3]; [4], [5]; [6], [7], [8]). These models demonstrate remarkable success in capturing neural responses and cognitive functions. Yet a model’s value as an account of the brain rests on more than reproducing such responses. A model of learning in the brain is biologically plausible only if biology could actually produce it—a property we call biological realizability. Realizability is not a verdict to be read off surface resemblance, nor a checklist of forbidden mechanisms to avoid; it is a demonstration that a mechanism could come to exist. And because biological structure has no external designer, any mechanism that realizes it must, at bottom, organize itself. Realizability is therefore fundamentally a question of self-organization—and, crucially, a matter of degree.
This reframing turns biological plausibility from a binary verdict into a graded axis (Figure 1): learning models differ in how much of their structure is stipulated in advance versus shown to emerge from local interactions. The axis runs deep. One may ask only that the weights self-organize, while the circuit that adjusts them is designed; or, going further, that the learning circuit itself assemble; further still, the scaffold that hosts it; and in principle, the developmental program that lays the scaffold down. The further down a model reaches, the more of what others simply assume it must instead derive—to move down the axis is to replace assumption with assembly. By this measure, most existing learning algorithms sit very near the top: they self-organize their inference weights, but under machinery that is otherwise fixed in advance. This is true even when training yields rich, interpretable structure—reusable dynamical motifs [9] or inhibitory microcircuits supporting working memory maintenance [10]—because such structure emerges in the inference weights being trained, while the learning rule that adjusts them remains designed.
Figure 1: A model is biologically plausible only if biology could produce it on its own: biological plausibility/realizability as a graded axis of self-organization. Learning algorithms differ in how much of their structure self-assembles from local interactions rather than being stipulated in advance. The vertical axis is an ordinal ladder of organizational levels: the inference/forward weights, the circuit that adjusts them, the scaffold that hosts it, and beyond. We label the second level the credit-assignment circuit to be concrete, though more generally it is the learning circuit—the machinery that turns experience into inference weight changes, of which credit assignment is the canonical example. Backprop and feedback-alignment variants self-organize essentially only their weights, leaving this circuit fixed; SAM assembles the circuit itself from uniform rules, resting only on a minimal two-stream scaffold whose form is largely required for stable perception. The faded top indicates that the axis is open-ended.
Nowhere is this stipulation more evident than in backpropagation, the standard
method for computing gradients in stochastic gradient descent (SGD). It
presupposes a precisely aligned architecture—forward and backward pathways tied
together by weight transport and neuron-level correspondence—that is difficult
to reconcile with biology [11, 5, 6].
Biologically plausible alternatives loosen parts of this machinery: feedback
alignment drops weight symmetry [12, 11], while weight
mirroring and the Kolen–Pollack rule let the backward weights adapt
[13]. Yet each still wires in a fixed one-to-one correspondence
between the two pathways rather than letting it form, and, more broadly, assumes
the prior existence of a specific learning-capable circuit that has not been
shown to be biologically assemblable
[14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Such models explain how a fixed
circuit can learn, but not how the circuit itself arises—presupposing the very
structure whose origin is in question. We call this unresolved gap the
architectural circularity of biologically plausible learning. A detailed
discussion of related approaches is provided in Section 2.
Stepping back from specific algorithmic proposals, we may instead ask a more
fundamental question: if biological learning systems are not globally designed,
what principles must govern their organization? Such a system must consist of
many interacting components, each following local rules, yet collectively giving
rise to coherent learning behavior at the system level. The problem is therefore
not to design a learning algorithm top-down, as in previous attempts, but to
understand how learning structure itself can emerge from distributed local
interactions.
This perspective leads us to formulate a scientific problem we call
Self-Assembling Learning (SAL): how systems composed of interacting parts,
governed only by local interaction rules rather than centralized design, can
emerge from lower-level interactions into structures that implement effective
learning algorithms. Biological neural circuits provide a particularly rich and
concrete instantiation of this principle, though the SAL formulation is more
general and applies to any distributed system in which learning must emerge
rather than be imposed.
Addressing SAL requires bridging multiple levels of analysis. At the
computational level, one must specify what objective a self-organizing system
ultimately optimizes; at the algorithmic level, the dynamical process by which
parameters are updated; at the implementation level, the physical mechanisms
through which such dynamics are realized. These explanatory levels must in turn
connect to levels of biological organization—molecular processes, synaptic
interactions, circuit motifs, large-scale networks, and distributed systems.
These are, in effect, the same levels along which the self-organization axis
runs: to ask how far self-assembly reaches is to ask how many of these levels
emerge from local rules rather than being assumed.
The central challenge of SAL is therefore to understand how local mechanisms at
lower levels of organization can give rise to algorithmic learning dynamics at
higher levels, which in turn implement coherent computational objectives. In
neural systems, this means explaining how synaptic plasticity and cellular
dynamics can self-organize into circuits whose collective behavior resembles
principled optimization.
As a constructive step toward this goal, we introduce a circuit building block,
the Self-Assembling Motif (SAM). SAM consists of four synaptic pathways
connecting two interacting information streams, all governed by uniform
heterosynaptic plasticity rules. SAM is self-assembling at two scales: first, the
motif itself emerges from initially random connectivity under local plasticity
rules; second, when repeated hierarchically across layers, networks of SAMs
self-organize into structured learning dynamics. In particular, the one-to-one
correspondence between pathways that prior methods wire in is, in SAM, not
assumed: its cross-stream connections are themselves randomly
initialized and plastic, so the alignment between the two streams forms on its own.
SAM is not assumption-free: it takes as given the existence of two
interacting streams with only modulatory—not driving—interactions between
them. This assumption is not arbitrary. Two segregated streams whose
cross-interactions cannot drive one another are close to what any system
performing stable perception requires: strong driving connections across streams
would form closed driving loops, leading to runaway amplification and the
contamination of inference by error signals. The two-stream scaffold is thus
better understood as a stability constraint that bounds the space of viable
architectures than as a designed blueprint, and it is exactly the kind of
low-information prior that development can plausibly supply. Within these minimal
constraints, everything in SAM that performs learning—the credit-assignment
circuit and all of its connections—self-assembles from initially random
connectivity under uniform local rules. SAM therefore sits further down the self-organization axis (Figure 1) than any prior biologically plausible learning algorithm.
We theoretically prove that, up to a measurable approximation error, hierarchically composed SAM networks implement a generalized form of SGD whose effective learning rate is a matrix that itself emerges through plasticity and is asymptotically positive semidefinite. The emergence of such gradient dynamics from purely local rules shows
that optimization behavior can arise without top-down architectural
specification, making gradient-based learning compatible with biological
self-organization—and to a far greater extent than previously thought.
2 Related Work
Our work is not the first to propose biologically plausible alternatives to backpropagation [11, 12, 14, 16, 15]. Recent efforts explore a variety of directions — top-down weight alignment [20], pseudoinverse feedback [21], and meta-learned plasticity rules over random feedback [22], as well as objective-minimization accounts such as the free-energy principle [23]. The most closely related approach to ours is the Kolen-Pollack algorithm [24, 13], a special case of our circuit in which the cross-connections are frozen and initialized to the identity. Very recently, [25] showed how learning can be integrated with other visual functions, such as visual guidance, by exploiting the interplay of ascending and descending cortical pathways. Below we examine several approaches in more detail, in two categories ordered by biological grounding.
2.1 ML-style biologically plausible learning
A substantial body of work (see [5, 6] for comprehensive reviews) has sought to make gradient-based learning more biologically plausible by modifying some specific aspects of backpropagation while retaining most of its machine learning oriented designs. Feedback alignment and its variants replace exact weight symmetry with fixed and random feedback connections [11, 12].
Such approaches successfully eliminate the requirement of exact weight transport, but they typically rely on an artificial one-to-one correspondence between forward and backward neurons or pathways. The backward structure is assumed to mirror the forward structure and often derivatives of forward activations are re-used during the backward pass.
Direct feedback alignment and related methods [14, 15] further relax the architectural symmetry constraints in forward and backward pathways. However, they still keep the global constraints such as fixed and non-plastic feedback pathways, and the overall credit-assignment structure is specified a priori rather than emerging through biological assembly. Contrary to the feedback alignment philosophy, there is substantial evidence that biological synapses are highly plastic in feedback pathways during adulthood (see section 3.7 for detailed discussion).
Finally, these models are typically formulated at the algorithmic level and do not provide detailed correspondence to specific biological cell types or microcircuit organization.
2.2 Models with stronger biological grounding
More recent work has pursued tighter correspondence with cortical microcircuit structure by embedding credit assignment mechanisms directly within biologically grounded neuronal models. Examples include burst-based models such as BurstProp [18] and BurstCCN [19], as well as two-compartment neuron models such as Error-encoding Dendritic Networks (EDNs) [17] and [20].
Unlike feedback alignment methods, these approaches attempt to resolve the artificial one-to-one duplication of forward and backward neurons by allowing feedback signals to propagate through the original feedforward neurons themselves. We refer to this strategy as original-pathway feedback. Rather than introducing a separate backward population that mirrors the forward pathway, credit assignment signals are multiplexed onto the same neurons that transmit activations.
Two interesting mechanisms have been proposed within these models. In burst-based models, bursts are treated as distinct from ordinary spikes, enabling activation and error signals to coexist within the same neuronal pathway. This activation–error multiplexing is an elegant conceptual solution to the alignment problem. However, it assumes that bursts encode error-specific information, a hypothesis that remains to be firmly established biologically.
Alternatively, in models such as EDN, small perturbations or “nudges” are introduced to neuronal activations, allowing discrepancies to propagate across layers through multi-compartment dynamics. While this avoids explicit weight transport, the approach may be sensitive to other sources of activity fluctuation. Moreover, in its current formulation, EDN retains a one-to-one correspondence between interneurons in layer
kk and pyramidal neurons in layer k+1k+1, requiring a somewhat artificial alignment at the circuit level.
Importantly, although these models achieve detailed biological correspondence at the cellular and dendritic levels, they generally assume the existence of a learning-capable microcircuit. The motif responsible for credit assignment is specified rather than shown to emerge through local assembly processes. As a result, they do not fully address the architectural circularity underlying the origin of learning-capable structure.
In contrast, our approach employs what may be termed other-pathway feedback, in which error signals propagate through distinct pathways that do not reuse the original feedforward neurons as intermediate carriers. This separation avoids activation–error multiplexing and does not require distinguishing bursts from spikes. More importantly, the feedback-supporting circuit motif itself is self-assembling under local plasticity rules, addressing both credit assignment and architectural emergence within a unified framework. Biology may employ either or both strategies, but here we focus on end-to-end self-assemblability of the learning architecture.

3.1 A neural circuit architecture that self-assembles
We propose a new ANN circuit architecture that is grounded in simple, biological synaptic motifs (Fig. 2). It is based on two interacting streams of information flow: an ascending stream representing forward pathways and a descending stream representing feedback pathways, mirroring cortical connectivity between areas. These streams are defined by a set of synaptic weights, which are subject to local heterosynaptic plasticity rules. Unlike the standard computer implementation of gradient descent – backpropagation – our model does not require the explicit computation of derivatives, and does not require weight symmetry between forward and feedback pathways.
There are a number of key features that differentiate our new ANNs from other approaches:
•
Heterosynaptic Learning Rules: All synaptic updates are driven by the activity of the synapse itself and of other synapses on the same neuron. The latter do not need to be electrically close to the modifiable synapse, depending on the specific mechanism of heterosynaptic plasticity.
•
Biological Congruence: The architecture aligns with known anatomical and physiological properties of cortical circuits.
•
Reduced Assumptions: Our models do not require any weight symmetry and still achieve competitive performance on challenging datasets like CIFAR-10;
•
Structural Resilience: Our circuit motif works with a range of nonlinearities underlying the transduction from synaptic activities to neuron activity (and corresponding to the ReLU nonlinearity used in many neural networks) and for an arbitrary width of the forward and backward pathway. The network is therefore robust to small changes in the local configurations of pathways.

Remark 1.
Theorem 1 separates two alignment notions.
Interstream alignment (Evℓ→0E_{v}^{\ell}\to 0) is proved unconditionally when ηV=ηV¯\eta_{V}=\eta_{\bar{V}}: the streams become transpose-matched at each splice, V¯ℓ−1→(Vℓ)T\bar{V}^{\ell-1}\to(V^{\ell})^{T}.
Pathway alignment (EpℓE_{p}^{\ell} small) is the conditional hypothesis under which the dual-stream motif implements generalized SGD with matrix learning rate HDℓ+E~v,DℓH_{D}^{\ell}+\widetilde{E}_{v,D}^{\ell}.
Empirically ‖Epℓ‖\|E_{p}^{\ell}\| and ‖Epℓ‖/‖ℱℓ‖\|E_{p}^{\ell}\|/\|\mathcal{F}^{\ell}\| often decrease during stable training, but this is not proved from the rules alone; when and why EpℓE_{p}^{\ell} improves or instead increases (as in diverging runs) is left to future work (Figure 4).
Early in training, finite ϵvk\epsilon_{v}^{k} inflates the depth-chain residual before EvE_{v} decays; as E~v,Dℓ\widetilde{E}_{v,D}^{\ell} and RℓR^{\ell} shrink, the update approaches SGD preconditioned by the learned matrix HDℓH_{D}^{\ell}, which is positive semidefinite and therefore does not oppose the SGD gradient.
It is worth noticing that this algorithm is different from SGD. The effective learning rate on WℓW^{\ell} is the matrix HDℓ+E~v,DℓH_{D}^{\ell}+\widetilde{E}_{v,D}^{\ell}, not a scalar: it is explicitly time-dependent and is itself learned by the algorithm. In the aligned limit this reduces to the positive semidefinite matrix HDℓH_{D}^{\ell}. This is consistent with observed metaplasticity in biological systems ([27]; [28]) – though it extends the idea in a fundamental way since the learning algorithm itself is being learned during the training of the forward network. In Figure 3a, we show examples of the HH matrix after training.
Another interesting aspect of the algorithm (see Theorem 1) is that it allows (but does not require) the backward pathway to be overparametrized. That is, the number of downstream connections can be larger than the number of forward connections. This matches observations in biological circuits ([29]), in which feedback connections can greatly outnumber feedforward ones—a flexibility that most prior biologically inspired learning algorithms do not accommodate because they require matched pathway sizes. Also, the backward pathway is flexible in the choice of DdD_{d}; the alignment framework and Theorem 1 apply for the Dd=ID_{d}=I case used here and more generally.

3.3 SAM works efficiently in learning
We evaluate our algorithm on seven widely used image classification datasets: CIFAR10 ([30]), MNIST ([31]), Fashion MNIST ([32]), ChestMNIST ([33], [34]), PathMNIST ([33], [34]), SVHN ([35]), and STL10 ([36]). For details about these datasets, see SI Experimental Setup. We compare our algorithm’s performance against the standard backpropagation and other biologically plausible algorithms. These include Feedback Alignment (FA) ([12]), which uses random fixed weights for error propagation instead of transposed weights; Weight Mirroring (WM) ([13]), which learns transposed feedback weights from noise in a separate phase; and the KP algorithm ([13, 24]), which gives forward and feedback weights the same local update and weight decay, so they become transposes of each other during training. While the primary goal of this paper is not to pursue State-of-the-Art (SOTA) performance but to design a neural network algorithm that can be deployed in the brain, our approach achieves SOTA among biologically plausible algorithms on all datasets (see Table 1). Notably, our algorithm outperforms models trained with backpropagation on six out of the seven datasets. The only exception is ChestMNIST, where it performs 0.05% lower than backpropagation.
3.4 Overparameterization in the Downstream Pathway is Beneficial
In the nervous system, the number of backprojections can be much larger than that of the forward stream. For example, the feedback connections V1 to LGN can be roughly 10 times the RGC synapses from the retina ([29]). We have observed a potential computational explanation of this phenomenon in our simulations. Our models perform better as the feedback pathway becomes overparametrized and achieves the best performance across multiple datasets when the parameter count of the downstream pathway is approximately 5 to 8 times that of the upstream pathway. Specifically, as shown in Figure 3c for CIFAR10, performance peaks when the parameter count of the downstream pathway is 8 times that of the upstream pathway. We perform experiments on the three most challenging datasets from the seven datasets used in Table 1: CIFAR10 ([30]), SVHN ([35]), and STL10 ([36]).

3.6 Interconnection Plasticity is Crucial
A remaining question is whether learning at some of the interconnections is more important than at others, and if so, which is more important? Additionally, are any interconnections not important at all? We perform this ablation study on CIFAR-10, where we initialize all the parameters randomly but only update a subset of all the connections. See Figure 3b. Note that WW is always plastic. To gauge how much the network has learned about the nonlinear relations in the data, we also compare with the learning trajectory of a linear model trained with SGD.
We discover the following phenomena:
•
Only updating WW works but only works as well as the linear model; making other parts plastic is thus important for learning nonlinear functions;
•
If only one additional connection is made plastic, V¯\bar{V} is the most important one, improving performance from 40%40\% to roughly 46%46\%;
•
Making V¯\bar{V} plastic is similar to making W,W¯,VW,\ \bar{W},\ V plastic together, suggesting that some loss of learning capabilities due to connection lesions can be compensated by other connections (but not all);
•
Making all four connections plastic improves the performance further, to the SGD level (cf. Table 1); this means that all connections need to be plastic to achieve the best performance.
Therefore, none of the pathways we introduced are redundant, and this partially explains the outperformance of our proposed algorithm over the existing two-pathway biologically plausible learning algorithms. Another reflection of the fact that there is an asymmetry between different pathways is that different connections require different learning time constants to work. In particular, the V¯\bar{V} matrices require the largest learning rates, usually four times that of WW (see SI Experimental Setup).

3.7 How to identify these circuits
A key question is whether and how one can identify the circuit we proposed in biological systems, especially how one can experimentally distinguish it from other algorithms of the feedback alignment type. In essence, this broad family of algorithms has four pathways (uu, ud, dd, du): (1) u (upstream) →\to u, (2) u (upstream) →\to d (down stream), (3) d →\to d, (4) d →\to u. The difference between these feedback type algorithms is in their prediction of the (a) anatomical structure and (b) plasticity of each of the pathways. See Table 2. For example, a biological circuit is compatible with Feedback alignment (1) if uu and dd are randomly connected, and ud and du are not (which could happen, for example, in the cerebellum) and if (2) only uu is plastic, while dd, ud and du are not. In contrast, a biological circuit where all pathways are random and plastic will be compatible with our proposal, and not with the feedback alignment. Therefore, our proposal implies an eight-category classification of plausible learning algorithms, and biologists can experimentally test all eight criteria to decide which category applies to their system.
There should not be an a priori belief that biological brains only use one such algorithm throughout the brain, and it is fully possible that different parts of the brain use different types of connections. For example, the feedback alignment motif seems especially suitable for the Purkinje cells because the climbing fibers are one-to-one, but it is very unlikely to be the right motif for, say, the cortices. For example, the apical dendrites of the L5 neurons, receiving largely feedback inputs, are found to be very plastic during motor learning ([40]). Similarly, the axon boutons of L6 neurons are highly plastic, and these axons often attach onto the basal dendrites of the L5 neurons, which could provide a feedback signal ([40]). There is, in any case, substantial evidence that spines in layer 1 (where many feedback inputs make their synapses) on both L5 and L2/3 pyramidal neurons are reasonably plastic across the adult mouse cortex, and that they show enhanced plasticity during learning paradigms ([41]; [42]; [43]; [44]; [45]; [46]; [47]; [48]); at these same feedback-targeted compartments, [49] recently reported vectorized instructive signals at L5 apical dendrites during learning.
3.8 An Example Implementation of SAM in Cortex
Self-Assembling Motif (SAM) is defined in terms of four abstract pathway types—upstream-to-upstream (u→\tou), upstream-to-downstream (u→\tod), downstream-to-downstream (d→\tod), and downstream-to-upstream (d→\tou). These pathway types are functional constructs rather than claims about specific neuron morphologies or laminar structures. The defining architectural principles of SAM are (i) the existence of two interacting streams, (ii) driving interactions within each stream and modulatory interactions between streams, and (iii) the absence of closed loops composed entirely of driving connections within either stream. SAM itself makes no assumptions about the biophysical mechanisms by which these properties are implemented. When viewed through this functional lens, cortical lamination and its canonical connectivity patterns provide a useful and concrete example of how the SAM architecture could be realized in a biological system.
In sensory cortex, many pathways that operate within the same functional stream—such as those carrying representational content—are strong and driving, whereas interactions between distinct streams often exert a modulatory influence on activity and learning. Importantly, the distinction emphasized by SAM is not between feedforward and feedback pathways per se, but between within-stream and cross-stream interactions. Under this interpretation, a number of well-established cortical pathways can be organized into the four SAM categories. Table 3 summarizes one such correspondence between canonical cortical pathways and SAM pathway types, together with a hypothesized relationship between dendritic targeting and the resulting driving or modulatory effects observed in cortex. Francioni et al. [49] report vectorized instructive signals at L5 apical dendrites during learning; this is consistent with the apical/distal targeting of the d→ud\to u pathway in the table.
Interpretation and scope.
The correspondence shown in Table 3 should be interpreted as an example of how SAM could be implemented in cortex, rather than as a claim that SAM must be implemented through cortical lamination or that cortical lamination evolved specifically to implement SAM. Cortical areas exhibit substantial variation in laminar structure, connectivity, and cellular properties, and exceptions to canonical patterns are well documented. In particular, some cortical neurons, such as large layer 5 pyramidal neurons, exhibit conditional or gated driving as a result of their dendritic biophysics. Such properties reflect details of a specific biological implementation and are not assumptions or requirements of the SAM framework itself. Other brain systems may satisfy the same SAM constraints using different cellular or circuit mechanisms.
From the perspective of SAM, the central claim is therefore functional rather than morphological: learning systems that avoid unstable dynamics and signal contamination should exhibit two interacting streams, driving connectivity within each stream, modulatory connectivity between streams, and no closed loops of purely driving interactions within a stream. Cortical lamination provides one biologically plausible realization of these constraints, but not their only possible instantiation.

3.9 Self-Organization Beyond the Two-Stream Scaffold
The core of SAM is its local heterosynaptic learning rule; the two-stream motif
of the main text is only one setting in which we exercise it. In companion work
[50] we apply the same rule in a far more general setting—a
generalized SAM. A pool of neurons is randomly initialized and recurrently
connected, with no two-stream organization at all, and an evolutionary process
grows and prunes the connections. Driven by the same heterosynaptic plasticity,
the connectivity self-organizes into circuits that learn well, with the evolved
updates becoming aligned with the true gradient (see SI and [50]). This indicates that the heterosynaptic rule alone is enough to make a population of neurons self-organize into an effective learner, and that the SAL goal is achievable far beyond any specific scaffold—direct support for the broader SAL program.
4 Discussion
The theoretical and simulation results we present here demonstrate a new circuit architecture that not only emulates the computational abilities of conventional deep learning models but does so through mechanisms that align more closely with biological circuits. Specifically, our simulations show that a network trained with our algorithm achieves competitive performance on tasks such as image classification on CIFAR-10, closely matching or even surpassing traditional backpropagation systems in some cases. Theoretically, SAM implements generalized SGD when two explicit alignment conditions hold: interstream alignment Evℓ→0E_{v}^{\ell}\to 0 (proved unconditionally when ηV=ηV¯\eta_{V}=\eta_{\bar{V}}) and pathway alignment with ‖Epℓ‖\|E_{p}^{\ell}\| small—in successful runs typically observed to decrease, and empirically the dominant source of gradient-approximation error once EvE_{v} has tightened. Our proposed circuit does not pose any requirement on specificity of the connections. In fact, a surprising property of the architecture we propose is that the basic circuit self-assembles, emerging from random synaptic connections between the ascending and the descending stream. Intriguingly, the best computational performance is obtained when the number of backprojections is around 5 times the connections in the forward stream.
Optimization algorithms that can adjust the inner weights of a deep network are critical for state-of-the-art machine learning. This does not necessarily imply, however, that the same statement holds true for the brain. We have introduced a biologically plausible learning architecture that self-assembles from random initial connectivity and achieves SGD-like performance through local plasticity rules. Self-assembly requires an ascending and a descending stream – known to exist in all cortices – and initial random connections between them with synapses obeying reasonable local plasticity rules. It is also possible to use other biologically plausible versions of the regularization term we introduced, such as a Winner-Takes-All form of regularization. Such a mechanism can lead to one-to-one synapses between ascending and descending streams. It remains an open problem whether the cortex uses this mechanism. A brief discussion of the biological plausibility of the associated plasticity rules is presented in SI Biological Evidence for Synaptic Plasticity Rules. It is also possible that this circuit motif can be made more flexible and can potentially enable a large variety of motifs to learn; companion work [50] already explores this in a scaffold-free setting (SI section Gradient Learning Without a Two-Stream Scaffold).
Vectorized instructive signals in cortex. [49] recently reported vectorized instructive signals, including task error, at L5 apical dendrites during learning, with semi-independent somatic and dendritic computation; perturbing apical signaling disrupts learning. We take this as compatible with our example cortical SAM implementation (Table 3), where inter-areal feedback targets apical/distal compartments (d→ud\to u, L5→\toL1, modulatory).
Temporal implementation and eligibility traces. SAM is formulated in discrete time, with upstream inference preceding downstream error propagation and synaptic update. This ordering isolates the self-assembly mechanism and makes its connection to gradient learning analyzable, but it should not be read as a complete real-time biophysical model. The local rule requires only that a transient trace of each synapse’s forward pre- and postsynaptic activity remain available when the feedback signal arrives—an eligibility trace, rather than a sustained copy of the full forward state—while the cross-stream weight that delivers the feedback signal is standing circuitry. The timing requirements of such continuous-time, biologically plausible learning are analyzed in [51, 52, 53]. A fully continuous-time treatment of SAM is left to future work.
Inference and learning in the descending stream. The present implementation uses SAM in an error-feedback setting, with input supplied at one end of the motif and target or error information at the other. This is a useful controlled instantiation, but it is not a defining commitment of SAL/SAM. In cortex, descending pathways are recurrent and multiplexed, contributing to inference, attention, contextual modulation, and the resolution of sensory ambiguity. This motivates the broader aim of SAL: not to hand-specify one pathway as an inference stream and another as a special-purpose error-routing stream, but to let functional roles arise from uniform local rules. SAM is a step in this direction, because its two streams are homologous and governed by the same local plasticity principles; the present asymmetry comes from how the motif is driven, not from an internally distinct backward learning rule.
This perspective resonates with the motivation behind biologically inspired approaches such as predictive coding, which likewise seek to move beyond a simple input–error dichotomy by introducing predictive pathways and tying them to learning. SAL/SAM, however, aims at something more general: the goal is not to reproduce a particular cortical phenomenon—error assignment, or prediction—but to let pathways and their functional roles emerge, rather than assigning them by design.
Whether cortical feedback carries predictions, errors, or multiplexed combinations of them, and how such signals are read by other pathways, remains an open and substantial problem. Our claim is not that the present model already captures inference-bearing feedback, but that SAM provides a symmetric substrate in which inputs and errors are simply two kinds of signal that the motif does not handle differently. This opens a path: one could drive SAM with other signals in future work—for instance, to learn bidirectional symmetric associations—allowing the streams to acquire distinct representational, inferential, or credit-assignment roles. In the strongest form of the SAL ideal, even the predefined streams would be relaxed, with pathways, roles, and functional distinctions all emerging through self-organization. Companion work [50] takes a first step in this direction: with only the locus of error entry fixed, the same heterosynaptic rule in a randomly connected recurrent pool evolves credit-assignment circuitry without predefined inference or error-delivery streams (see SI). Further extending self-organization to fully multiplexed, inference-bearing feedback beyond the two-stream scaffold remains for future work, but these directions point to an appealing unification that the SAL perspective brings into view.
Why “evolution did it” is not enough for realizability. A tempting objection is that any biologically plausible circuit is realizable because evolution could have produced it. But realizability asks whether a proposed path guarantees the structure we observe: EE explains XX only if EE would actually produce XX. “Evolution produced it” is equally compatible with the circuit we found, a different circuit, or none at all; because it fits every outcome, it singles out none, and so establishes no causal link to the particular circuit in front of us. This is the same failure as weight transport—which asserts a correspondence with no path to it—and as passing a plausibility checklist, which shows only the absence of violations, not the presence of a path. SAM is offered as an actual path: the credit-assignment circuit assembles from random connectivity under uniform local rules, so what others assume, it derives.
Experimental predictions. Our results indicate that biologically plausible learning algorithms can rival established machine learning algorithms while respecting the brain’s biophysical constraints. Our work makes several predictions that are, at least in principle, experimentally verifiable: 1) Synaptic Motifs - Reciprocal synaptic connections between ascending and descending pathways with four plastic synapses (WW, W¯\bar{W}, VV, V¯\bar{V}) should be generally present in cortical networks; 2) Heterosynaptic Plasticity - Changes in synaptic strength should depend on the activity of other synapses (these synapses must be on the same neuron but do not need to be electrically close, depending on the specific mechanism of heterosynaptic plasticity); 3) Self-Assembly - The initially random connections between the two streams assemble over development/learning, guided by heterosynaptic plasticity rules; 4) Distinct Pathways - The back projections may exhibit different electrophysiological properties, such as nonlinearities depending on synaptic inputs, compared to feedforward pathways, also possibly with different time constants as the computational model is found to work the best when different connections have different learning time constants; 5) Relative size of the streams: Our results indicate that learning produces better performance when the feedback connections are more numerous than the feedforward. Experimental verification of these predictions may transform our understanding of learning in both biological and artificial systems.

Appendix B Biological Evidence for Synaptic Plasticity Rules
The learning rules corresponding to the SAM dynamics in the main text are not strictly Hebb. In fact, the classical Hebb rule is an example of homosynaptic plasticity, whereas the proposed algorithm requires the plasticity of neighboring synapses through heterosynaptic interactions. One of the first examples of heterosynaptic facilitation is in [54].
There is empirical evidence that reinforcement of a synapse based on its own input and nearby synaptic input can occur independently of large postsynaptic depolarization, often through localized biochemical signaling rather than direct electrical depolarization of the postsynaptic cell. In this case, synaptic plasticity is influenced by the activity of neighboring synapses without necessitating a strong postsynaptic action potential. A possible mechanism is Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP) with Local Modulation: in some forms of STDP, nearby synapses on the same dendrite can influence plasticity based on the timing of inputs. For example, the timing of presynaptic spikes at two nearby excitatory synapses can trigger local signaling cascades within the dendritic segment that reinforce or weaken synaptic strength without requiring a large postsynaptic potential. Another is calcium-dependent modulation: local increases in calcium concentration can drive synaptic reinforcement or weakening in a spatially restricted manner. When one synapse is active, it can cause a modest calcium influx that can extend to nearby synapses without significantly depolarizing the postsynaptic neuron. This local calcium increase can activate signaling pathways, such as CaMKII or other kinases, that modify the strength of nearby synapses proportionally to their own activity levels and those of neighboring inputs. A third possible mechanism is retrograde signaling, which involves retrograde messengers (e.g., endocannabinoids or nitric oxide) that are released by the postsynaptic neuron in response to local synaptic activity and can act on presynaptic terminals. These messengers can diffuse locally and affect only the nearby synapses that are also active, reinforcing or weakening them proportionally to their individual input activities. An interesting case study is by [55] on synaptic tagging in hippocampal neurons. It demonstrated that synapses can interact biochemically on a local level. When a strong input induces LTP at one synapse, it can create a "tag" that allows nearby synapses to capture plasticity-related proteins and reinforce their strength based on their activity, even if they don’t independently trigger large postsynaptic depolarization. These mechanisms allow synaptic connections to encode complex input patterns across local networks, effectively performing computations that go beyond simple, strict Hebbian principles33

There are a few different possibilities of how to implement them biologically. One special detail one needs to pay attention to is the time ordering of firing..
In the main text, we have mainly focused on the simplest type of regularization. A more biologically plausible regularization is perhaps a Winner-Take-All regularization, and this could be a direction of future research.
Biological evidence for competition among incoming synapses on a single neuron (or dendrite). Research does suggest a competitive process among synapses on the same dendritic branch, particularly when multiple inputs converge on a single location. This competition is thought to be governed by mechanisms of synaptic plasticity, where synapses that are frequently active and contribute to the cell’s firing are strengthened, while less active ones may weaken or even be pruned.
One well-documented example of this competition is seen in activity-dependent plasticity processes, such as Hebbian plasticity and synaptic tagging and capture. When neighboring synapses are active at the same time, they may compete for limited resources, like proteins that help stabilize and strengthen synaptic connections. This often results in the selective strengthening of certain synapses and the weakening of others, a process referred to as heterosynaptic plasticity. In this way, some synapses are favored to persist, while others may diminish over time if they fail to “compete” effectively. This competitive mechanism is believed to play a crucial role in refining neural circuits, helping to maintain efficient and effective synaptic networks by eliminating redundant or less useful connections, especially during development and learning.
Biological evidence for competition among synapses from a single neuron (or axon) onto different neurons. There is evidence supporting the idea of presynaptic competition between synapses originating from the same neuron when they connect to different target neurons.
One of the most studied examples of presynaptic competition is in motor neurons and their connections to muscle fibers, especially during development. Motor neurons initially form multiple synapses on various muscle fibers, but over time, a pruning process occurs, often resulting in a single, stable connection with one muscle fiber. This phenomenon, known as synaptic pruning, is a process where less active synapses are eliminated while only the more effective and frequently active connections are maintained and strengthened.
This presynaptic competition is driven by neuronal activity and molecular signals. Research on long-term synaptic plasticity (LTP and LTD) suggests that competing synapses can affect each other’s likelihood of survival, even when they connect to different cells. Synchronized activity at certain synapses tends to stabilize those connections, while less synchronized or less active synapses are gradually weakened and may be removed.
Presynaptic competition is essential not only for refining neural networks but also for developing functional connections between neurons. It ensures that only the most efficient and relevant connections are maintained, optimizing neural circuitry for effective function.
Appendix C Gradient Learning Without a Two-Stream Scaffold
At its core, SAM is a local heterosynaptic learning rule that makes a population
of neurons self-organize; the two interacting streams of the main text are one
particular setting in which we exercise this rule, not a defining commitment.
Companion work [50] applies the same rule in a much more general
setting—a generalized SAM—and asks how far the self-organization reaches when
the two-stream organization is removed entirely. A pool of neurons is randomly
initialized and recurrently connected, with no partition into separate streams;
every synapse follows the same local heterosynaptic rule; and an evolutionary
process grows and prunes the connections, selecting only on task performance. The
sole structure fixed in advance is where the error signal enters the circuit.
From this scaffold-free setting, effective learning circuits self-organize
(Fig. 5): driven by the heterosynaptic rule, the
connectivity reorganizes across generations into circuits that learn well, with
the evolved updates becoming increasingly aligned with the true gradient. Good
credit-assignment circuitry therefore self-assembles from a generic pool of
neurons, without any scaffold. We take this as evidence on two fronts: that the
heterosynaptic rule introduced in this work is powerful enough, on its own, to
make a population of neurons self-organize into an effective learner; and that the
SAL goal—learning structure emerging from local rules rather than from
design—is achievable across a broad range of settings, well beyond the
particular two-stream instantiation analyzed in the main text. In this sense the
result supports the general direction of SAL. We refer the reader to
[50] for the learning rule, the evolutionary algorithm, and the full
set of simulations.

Datasets.
We use seven datasets to evaluate our method. CIFAR-10 [30] consists of 60,000 color images in 10 different classes, with each image having a resolution of 32x32 pixels. The dataset is divided into 50,000 training images and 10,000 testing images. The classes are mutually exclusive and include common objects such as airplanes, cars, cats, and dogs.
The MNIST [31] contains 70,000 grayscale images of handwritten digits (0-9), each with a resolution of 28x28 pixels. The dataset is divided into 60,000 training images and 10,000 testing images. MNIST is known for its simplicity and has been a standard dataset for testing machine learning algorithms.
Fashion MNIST [32] contains 70,000 grayscale images of clothing items like T-shirts, trousers, shoes, and bags, with each image having a resolution of 28x28 pixels. The dataset is also split into 60,000 training and 10,000 testing images. Fashion MNIST is considered more challenging than MNIST due to the variability in the visual features of clothing items.
The SVHN [35] contains over 600,000 color images of digits (0-9), each with a resolution of 32x32 pixels. The dataset is divided into a training set of 73,257 images, a testing set of 26,032 images, and an additional 531,131 images for extra training. SVHN is challenging due to the varying digit sizes, orientations, and complex backgrounds.
The ChestMNIST [33, 34] comprises chest X-ray images for multi-label classification tasks. Derived from the NIH ChestX-ray14 dataset, it includes images annotated with 14 different pathological labels, such as pneumonia and emphysema. It is widely used in medical imaging studies to develop and evaluate machine learning models capable of diagnosing multiple conditions from X-ray images.
PathMNIST [33, 34] is a pathology image dataset designed for multi-class classification tasks. It originates from the NCT-CRC-HE-100K dataset and includes histological images of nine different types of tissue from colorectal cancer samples, such as colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelium and lymphocytes. This dataset is used primarily for training models to differentiate between various cancerous tissue types based on their histopathological features.
STL-10 [36] consists of 13,000 labeled images across 10 different classes and 100,000 unlabeled images. Each image has a resolution of 96x96 pixels, which is significantly larger than CIFAR-10 images, making the dataset more challenging. The dataset has a training set of 5,000 labeled images and a test set of 8,000 labeled images.
Baselines.
We compare our method with four baselines, including SGD and three biologically-motivated algorithms.
Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) is the standard optimization method that updates parameters iteratively using a subset of data to minimize the objective function.
Weight Mirroring [13] learns feedback weights that approximate the transpose of the forward weights without weight transport: in a separate mirroring phase, noise is injected into the forward neurons and the feedback weights are updated with a local Hebbian rule, so they come to mirror the forward weights.
Feedback Alignment [12] offers an alternative to traditional backpropagation by using random, fixed weights instead of transposed forward-pass weights to propagate error signals.
Kolen-Pollack (KP) algorithm [24, 13] learns feedback weights without weight transport by applying the same local update (transposed) and the same weight decay to the forward and feedback weights during training, so their difference decays exponentially and the feedback weights converge to the transpose of the forward weights.
Appendix E Alignment Diagnostics
This section reports CIFAR-10 SAL vs. backprop alignment diagnostics that test the theory in §A.
The runs follow Algorithm 2 with hyperparameters as below unless noted.
Two error budgets.
The diagnostics separate two alignment quantities from §A.
Interstream alignment (Evℓ→0E_{v}^{\ell}\to 0) is proved unconditionally when ηV=ηV¯\eta_{V}=\eta_{\bar{V}} (Lemma 1); any residual junction mismatch in simulations reflects finite training time, stochastic sampling, or numerical precision.
Pathway alignment (EpℓE_{p}^{\ell} small) is conditional: convergence Epℓ→0E_{p}^{\ell}\to 0 from the weight rules alone is not proved (Remark 2).
In stable runs we often observe ‖Epℓ‖\|E_{p}^{\ell}\| and ‖Epℓ‖/‖ℱℓ‖\|E_{p}^{\ell}\|/\|\mathcal{F}^{\ell}\| decrease on most layers; when a run becomes unstable these quantities can instead grow.
When and why EpℓE_{p}^{\ell} improves or worsens during training is left to future work.
Measured quantities.
For each layer ℓ\ell and sample xx we form the forward pathway operator ℱℓ​(x)=Vℓ​Duℓ​(x)​(Wℓ)T\mathcal{F}^{\ell}(x)=V^{\ell}D_{u}^{\ell}(x)(W^{\ell})^{T}, the feedback pathway operator ℬℓ​(x)=Ddℓ​(x)​W¯ℓ​(V¯ℓ)T\mathcal{B}^{\ell}(x)=D_{d}^{\ell}(x)\bar{W}^{\ell}(\bar{V}^{\ell})^{T}, and the pathway error Epℓ​(x)=ℱℓ​(x)−ℬℓ​(x)E_{p}^{\ell}(x)=\mathcal{F}^{\ell}(x)-\mathcal{B}^{\ell}(x) (Definition 1).
Interstream alignment is proxied by junction mismatch |Vℓ+1−V¯ℓ​T||V^{\ell+1}-\bar{V}^{\ell\mathrm{T}}| and cosine similarity cos⁡(Vℓ+1,V¯ℓ​T)\cos(V^{\ell+1},\bar{V}^{\ell\mathrm{T}}).
We also log SAL vs. backprop gradient cosine similarity on WℓW^{\ell}, interstream gradient magnitudes |∇Vℓ||\nabla V^{\ell}| and |∇V¯ℓ||\nabla\bar{V}^{\ell}|, per-layer ‖Epℓ‖F\|E_{p}^{\ell}\|_{F}, and ‖Epℓ‖F/‖ℱℓ‖F\|E_{p}^{\ell}\|_{F}/\|\mathcal{F}^{\ell}\|_{F}; since Epℓ=ℱℓ−ℬℓE_{p}^{\ell}=\mathcal{F}^{\ell}-\mathcal{B}^{\ell}, the ratio expresses operator mismatch relative to the forward pathway scale and is comparable across layers.
V0V^{0} and W¯0\bar{W}^{0} are unused by the model yet enter Ep0E_{p}^{0}, and a fuller treatment is left to future work.
